## Narration (record as one paragraph)

What if you fell into a black hole? Bad news first… you're not coming back. But how it goes depends on which black hole you pick. A black hole packs so much stuff into so little space that not even light can escape. Its edge is called the event horizon… the point of no return. Fall toward a small one, about ten times the Sun's mass, and you'd never reach the edge in one piece. Gravity at your feet pulls way harder than at your head, so you'd stretch out like a noodle. Scientists really call this spaghettification. But a giant one, like the four-million-sun monster at the center of our galaxy, is gentler at the edge. You could float across the event horizon… and not feel a thing. Your friend watching from far away sees something weird. You slow down, turn red, and fade, like you froze in time. Near a black hole, time really slows down. In a NASA simulation, a six-hour flyby that skims the edge and escapes comes home thirty-six minutes younger. Once you're inside? NASA's math gives you about thirteen seconds before the stretching wins. What's at the very center… nobody knows. Comment NEXT and pick the next topic.

## Fact notes

- A black hole's gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, escapes once inside the event horizon. As of Nov 14, 2026. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ (checked: https://www.astronomy.com/science/ask-astro-why-are-small-black-holes-more-dangerous-than-big-ones/)
- Spaghettification: the pull on the near end of a falling object is much stronger than on the far end, so it stretches "like noodles". For a stellar-mass black hole (about 10 Suns) this tears you apart well before the horizon; for a supermassive one the stretch at the horizon is small, so you'd cross it intact (by our own estimate, the head-to-feet difference at Sgr A*'s horizon is about a thousandth of Earth's gravity). As of Nov 14, 2026. Sources: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/supermassive-black-holes/new-nasa-black-hole-visualization-takes-viewers-beyond-the-brink/ and https://www.discovermagazine.com/what-would-happen-if-you-fell-into-a-black-hole-41727 (checked: https://www.astronomy.com/science/ask-astro-why-are-small-black-holes-more-dangerous-than-big-ones/ — horizon tidal pull ≈19.6 billion m/s² for a stellar-mass black hole vs ≈0.0000019 m/s² for a supermassive one)
- "Not feel a thing" means gravity alone at the edge; the hot gas around some black holes would be deadly anyway. Keep it as "not feel a thing" (cartoon level), don't add more.
- Sagittarius A* (center of the Milky Way): about 4.3 million Suns, about 27,000 light-years away. As of Nov 14, 2026. Sources: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/supermassive-black-holes/new-nasa-black-hole-visualization-takes-viewers-beyond-the-brink/ (uses 4.3 million Suns, "equivalent to the monster at the center of the Milky Way") and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagittarius_A* (checked: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208/). The narration rounds 4.3 million to "four-million-sun".
- Seen from far away, a falling object's light gets redder and dimmer, it seems to slow down and fade, and it never visibly crosses the horizon; the falling person notices nothing special at the horizon. As of Nov 14, 2026. Source: https://www.astronomy.com/science/ask-astro-can-an-observer-ever-see-something-fall-into-a-black-hole/ (checked: https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2443)
- NASA's 2024 visualizations (Jeremy Schnittman, NASA Goddard): on a 6-hour round-trip flyby that escapes, an astronaut would return 36 minutes younger than colleagues far away; on the plunge, once the camera crosses the horizon, spaghettification is 12.8 seconds away. As of Nov 14, 2026 (NASA release May 6, 2024). Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/supermassive-black-holes/new-nasa-black-hole-visualization-takes-viewers-beyond-the-brink/ (checked: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1043682 and https://earthsky.org/space/plunge-into-a-black-hole-video-nasa/). Both numbers are for that simulated 4.3-million-Sun black hole.
- What's at the center (the "singularity") isn't understood; our current physics breaks down there. As of Nov 14, 2026. Source: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/ (checked: https://www.popsci.com/story/space/inside-a-black-hole-space/)
- Bonus (not in the narration): the nearest known black hole, Gaia BH1, is about 1,560 light-years away and about 10 Suns. As of Nov 14, 2026. Sources: https://keckobservatory.org/nearest-black-hole/ and https://www.sciencenews.org/article/closest-known-black-hole-to-earth
- Photos: NASA visualization stills are free to use with credit (NASA media guidelines; NASA Goddard Scientific Visualization Studio, https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/14576/). The Sgr A* image is from the Event Horizon Telescope via ESO, CC BY 4.0, credit "EHT Collaboration": https://www.eso.org/public/images/eso2208-eht-mwa/
