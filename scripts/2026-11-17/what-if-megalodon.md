What if Megalodon were still alive? Megalodon was the biggest shark that ever lived. Its teeth grew up to seven inches long… bigger than your hand. Big ones reached about fifty feet, and a 2025 study says the very biggest may have hit eighty… about two school buses. Its bite was six to ten times stronger than a great white's. Fossil whale bones show its bite marks. So if it lived today, whales would be on the menu. And it would be hungry all the time. A 2023 study found Megalodon was partly warm-blooded, about seven degrees Celsius warmer than the water around it. A warm body burns a lot of food. Its babies grew up in warm, shallow water near the shore. But here's the twist. Sharks lose teeth their whole lives, so Megalodon dropped teeth all over the world… and the youngest ones are about three point six million years old. Nothing newer. That's how scientists know it's gone. What finished it off? A cooling ocean, less food… and maybe a smaller rival: the great white shark. Scientists still argue which mattered most. ## Fact notes

- Island: 49 birds (YouTube 25, TikTok 17, Instagram 4, Facebook 3), so one more follower = 50; the bird bath (viewer @dakotaboyd4809's idea) is being built; the Secret Building is under wraps; the "? NEXT BUILD" plot is open. As of Nov 17, 2026. Source: `town.json`, `village/island-news.md`, project notes. Nothing was under "To announce" in island-news.md.
- Megalodon (Otodus megalodon) is the largest shark known; teeth up to about 18 cm (7 in); an average tooth is about 5 in. As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/megalodon--the-truth-about-the-largest-shark-that-ever-lived.html (checked: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-of-natural-history/2019/07/29/megalodon-may-be-extinct-theres-life-size-one-smithsonian/)
- Size: a 2025 study (Shimada and 28 co-authors, Palaeontologia Electronica) estimated a nearly complete Belgian backbone at about 16.4 m (54 ft) and the largest Danish specimen at about 24.3 m (80 ft), "the largest possible reasonable estimate". The narration says "big ones reached about fifty feet" and "the very biggest may have hit eighty". A school bus is about 35–45 ft long, so 80 ft ≈ two buses. As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://www.depaul.edu/news/megalodon-study-2025 (checked: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1075419 and https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/megalodon-shark-bigger-size-slim-fossil)
- Bite: estimated 108,514–182,201 N vs about 18,216 N for a great white (a model scaled up from great whites, not a measurement) = about 6–10 times stronger. As of Nov 17, 2026. Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/megalodon--the-truth-about-the-largest-shark-that-ever-lived.html (checked: https://www.livescience.com/facts-about-megalodon.html)
- Whale bite marks: fossil whale bones and a sperm whale tooth carry bite marks from megalodon (or its close relative); scientists can't always tell attack from scavenging, so the narration only says "bite marks". As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://www.livescience.com/megalodon-shark-attacks-whale-fossils.html (checked: https://www.livescience.com/ancient-shark-bite-whale-flipper)
- Warm-blooded: PNAS, June 26, 2023 (Griffiths et al., with Shimada): average body temperature about 27 °C, about 7 °C warmer than the surrounding water ("regional endothermy", like great whites and makos, not like whales); the authors say it needed constant food, which may have made it vulnerable. As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://www.depaul.edu/news/shimada-megalodon-june23 (checked: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/megalodons-were-warm-blooded-and-it-was-a-blessing-and-a-curse-180982438/)
- Nurseries: young megalodons grew up in warm, shallow coastal nurseries (first found in Panama's Gatun Formation, about 10 million years old). As of Nov 17, 2026. Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0010552 (Pimiento et al., PLoS ONE, 2010) (checked: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/megalodon--the-truth-about-the-largest-shark-that-ever-lived.html)
- Extinct about 3.6 million years ago (the youngest fossils); sharks replace teeth throughout life, which is why fossil teeth are common worldwide. As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2022/may/great-white-sharks-could-have-contributed-megalodon-extinction.html (checked: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/megalodon--the-truth-about-the-largest-shark-that-ever-lived.html)
- Why it died out: climate cooling and falling prey numbers probably played a role; a 2022 zinc-isotope study (Nature Communications) found early great whites ate at a similar level, so competition may have contributed; the authors call it preliminary. As of Nov 17, 2026. Sources: https://www.nsf.gov/news/great-white-sharks-may-have-contributed-megalodon-extinction (checked: https://www.livescience.com/great-white-sharks-megalodon-extinction)
- Photos: use only free-to-use images (Wikimedia Commons with license, or a museum image that allows reuse) with a credit line. Say honestly what a photo shows (a fossil tooth, not a living shark).
